# Kerosene: The Fuel Between Gasoline and Diesel

Kerosene is a **flammable liquid fuel derived from petroleum**, made up of a mixture of hydrocarbons. It occupies an intermediate range in the petroleum refining process: generally less volatile than gasoline and lighter than many diesel-range fractions.

Although it is commonly associated with household lamps and cooking stoves, kerosene also powers commercial aircraft and, in a highly refined form, some of the world's most powerful rockets.

## 1. How Is Kerosene Made?

Kerosene is produced during the refining of crude oil. The process involves separating and treating different hydrocarbon mixtures to obtain fuels with specific properties.

### Step 1: Fractional distillation

Crude oil is heated and fed into a distillation column. As its components vaporize and rise through the column, they separate according to their boiling ranges.

Kerosene is collected as a middle distillate, typically within a boiling range of approximately **150–250°C**, although the exact range depends on the refinery and product specification.

### Step 2: Refining and treatment

The separated kerosene fraction may undergo additional treatment, including **hydrotreating**, in which hydrogen is used under elevated pressure and temperature to reduce sulfur and other unwanted compounds.

Some refineries use additional processes to improve properties such as odor, color, stability and combustion quality.

### Step 3: Meeting fuel specifications

The finished product is tested and, where necessary, further processed or blended to meet the requirements of its intended use. Aviation fuel, for example, must satisfy strict specifications for freezing point, combustion performance and other properties.

## 2. Types of Kerosene

Kerosene is not a single, uniform product. Different grades are manufactured to meet different operating conditions and safety requirements.

|Grade|Typical application|Key characteristic|
|---|---|---|
|Jet A|Commercial aviation, primarily in the United States|Kerosene-type aviation fuel with a maximum freezing point of −40°C|
|Jet A-1|Commercial aviation internationally|Similar to Jet A, but with a lower maximum freezing point of −47°C|
|No. 1-K|Domestic heating and suitable wick-fed appliances|A regulated grade of kerosene with specified properties|
|No. 2-K|Certain domestic heating appliances|A different regulated kerosene grade, with requirements distinct from No. 1-K|
|RP-1|Rocket propulsion|Highly refined rocket-grade kerosene designed for use with liquid oxygen|

**An important distinction:** Jet A and Jet A-1 are aviation fuel specifications, while No. 1-K and No. 2-K are heating-fuel grades. RP-1 is specially refined for rocket engines; ordinary household kerosene is not an interchangeable substitute.

_Sources: [ExxonMobil — Jet A and Jet A-1 fuel specifications](https://www.exxonmobil.com/en-in/commercial-fuel/pds/gl-xx-jetfuel-series); [U.S. EIA — Kerosene](https://www.eia.gov/tools/glossary/index.php?id=kerosene)._

## 3. Physical and Chemical Properties

Kerosene's properties make it useful in applications that require a liquid fuel with relatively low volatility and substantial energy content.

|Property|Approximate value or description|
|---|---|
|Physical state|Liquid at ordinary room temperature|
|Appearance|Clear to pale yellow, depending on composition and grade|
|Boiling range|Approximately 150–250°C for typical kerosene fractions; product specifications vary|
|Flash point|Generally above 38°C for many kerosene grades; the precise minimum depends on the grade and test method|
|Energy content by volume|Approximately 35 MJ/L, depending on composition and grade|
|Chemical composition|A mixture of hydrocarbons, rather than a single chemical compound|
|Volatility|Lower than gasoline under comparable conditions|

### Why is kerosene less volatile than gasoline?

Kerosene generally contains larger hydrocarbon molecules than gasoline. These molecules tend to require more energy to enter the vapor phase, resulting in a higher boiling range and lower volatility.

This can make kerosene less prone to forming ignitable vapors than gasoline under comparable conditions. However, **kerosene remains flammable** and must be stored and handled appropriately.

A related property is the _flash point_: the lowest temperature, under a specified test method, at which a liquid produces enough vapor to ignite momentarily when exposed to an ignition source.

## 4. What Is Kerosene Used For?

### Aviation

Jet A and Jet A-1 are kerosene-type fuels used in commercial aircraft turbine engines. Their specifications make them suitable for the temperature and operating conditions encountered during flight.

### Cooking, heating and lighting

Kerosene has long been used in space heaters, cooking stoves and wick-fed lamps. These applications remain important in areas where other energy sources are unavailable or less accessible.

### Industrial applications

Certain kerosene fractions and related petroleum products are used as solvents, cleaning fluids or process materials. Suitability depends on the product's formulation and the intended application.

### Rocket propulsion

Rocket-grade kerosene, known as **RP-1**, is used with liquid oxygen (LOX) in liquid-propellant rocket engines.

RP-1 is not simply ordinary kerosene poured into a rocket. It is manufactured to meet much more demanding requirements for consistency and engine performance.

## 5. The Space Connection: Kerosene That Reaches Orbit

One of the most interesting applications of kerosene is rocket propulsion.

SpaceX's **Falcon 9** uses nine Merlin engines in its first stage. These engines burn RP-1 with liquid oxygen, producing the thrust required to lift the rocket from the launch pad.

Why use kerosene instead of a more familiar fuel?

- **High energy density:** A substantial amount of chemical energy can be stored in a relatively compact volume of liquid fuel.
    
- **Liquid at ordinary temperatures:** RP-1 does not require the extreme cryogenic storage conditions needed for liquid hydrogen.
    
- **Established engineering:** Kerosene-based propulsion has a long history in launch vehicles, including the Saturn V's first stage.
    

The key is that a rocket must carry both fuel and an oxidizer. Unlike an aircraft engine, which can obtain oxygen from the atmosphere, a rocket must carry its own oxidizer to operate in space.

In the Falcon 9's first stage, that oxidizer is liquid oxygen.

## 6. A Few Things Worth Remembering

- Kerosene is a **mixture of hydrocarbons**, not one pure chemical substance.
    
- Its properties vary according to its grade, refining process and intended use.
    
- A higher flash point than gasoline does not mean that kerosene is non-flammable or safe to handle carelessly.
    
- Jet fuel and rocket-grade kerosene are specialized fuels that must meet specific performance requirements.
    
- RP-1 and liquid oxygen form a powerful rocket propellant combination used by the Falcon 9.
